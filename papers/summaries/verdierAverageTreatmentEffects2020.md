# Average Treatment Effects for Stayers with Correlated Random Coefficient Models of Panel Data

## Bibliographic header

Valentin Verdier (Department of Economics, University of North Carolina - Chapel Hill). Draft dated May 25, 2020; published in the Journal of Applied Econometrics. Online appendix at https://bit.ly/3fGURJe.

## Research question

When a correlated random coefficient (CRC) panel model is used to recover heterogeneous returns to a binary treatment, difference-in-differences identifies the average treatment effect (ATE) only for "movers" (units whose treatment status changes over the panel); the ATE for "stayers" (always-treated or never-treated units) is left unrestricted. What additional restrictions are needed to extrapolate from movers to stayers, how restrictive are they, and can the extrapolation be tested? Can a generalized extrapolation accommodate factors of selection into treatment that originate from observed sources and would otherwise invalidate the simple extrapolation?

## Audience

Applied econometricians and empirical researchers using panel data with heterogeneous treatment effects --- particularly those building on Chamberlain (1982, 1992), Lemieux (1998), Suri (2011), Graham and Powell (2012), and the recent literature on heterogeneous-effects difference-in-differences (Abadie 2005; de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfoeuille 2018a,b; Callaway and Sant'Anna 2018; Goodman-Bacon 2018). Highly relevant to researchers estimating generalized Roy/GRC models of self-selection with panel data, where identification for non-switchers is at stake.

## Method

The paper is a methodological contribution with an empirical illustration. Verdier (i) shows the Lemieux (1998) and Suri (2011) methods can be represented as a linear extrapolation from diff-in-diff estimates, (ii) characterizes the restriction embedded in the "simple" extrapolation assumption --- namely, that $E(a_i | b_i, c_{i1},\ldots,c_{iT})$ be linear in $b_i$ and that cost shifters $c_{it}$ be independent of $(a_i, b_i)$, (iii) develops a "robust" extrapolation that accommodates correlated cost shifters when they originate from an observed indexing variable $v_i$, (iv) provides two-step estimation (first-step OLS producing noisy individual-level $\hat{a}_i, \hat{b}_i$; second-step IV or FE-IV regression of $\hat{a}_i$ on $\hat{b}_i$ among movers using treatment-status history as instruments), (v) shows the extrapolation identifying assumption can be tested as a GMM overidentification test when $T \ge 3$, and (vi) applies the procedures to Kenyan maize-farmer panel data to estimate returns to hybrid seed adoption.

## Data

Longitudinal data from the Tegemeo Agricultural Monitoring and Policy Analysis (TAMPA) Project on Kenyan maize farmers, waves 1997, 2004, 2007, and 2010. Same data source as Suri (2011), which used only the 1997 and 2004 waves. Unbalanced panel: 1,130 farmers, 3,770 farmer-year observations (~3.5 waves per farmer). 354 movers, 123 untreated stayers (never used hybrid seeds), 653 treated stayers (always used hybrid seeds). Controls $z_{it}$: main-season rainfall, other inputs, acres planted, household demographics, and province-by-year fixed effects. Outcome $y_{it}$: log maize yield (kg/acre). Treatment $x_{it}$: use of hybrid seeds.

## Statistical methods

### CRC model

The paper estimates the CRC model $y_{it} = a_i + f_t + b_i x_{it} + u_{it}$ (with optional additive $z_{it} g_0$ for controls), under strict exogeneity $E(u_{d,it}|X) = 0$ and additive separability of $a_i + f_t$ in baseline outcomes. Movers are $M_n = \{i : \min_t x_{it} = 0 \text{ and } \max_t x_{it} = 1\}$; the remaining units are stayers. With $T=2$ and the normalization $f_1 = 0$: differences in time effects are identified from stayers, $E(\Delta y_i | 0, 0) = \Delta f$; ATE for the two mover groups are identified by diff-in-diff, $E(b_i | 0, 1) = E(\Delta y_i | 0, 1) - \Delta f$ and symmetrically for $(1, 0)$; averages of $a_i$ are identified from the first-period outcome of untreated stayers and switchers-into-treatment; averages of $a_i + b_i$ from treated stayers.

### Simple (non-robust) extrapolation

Lemieux-Suri identifying assumption (2.11): $E(a_i \mid b_i, x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iT}) = E(a_i \mid b_i)$ and the conditional mean is linear in $b_i$ --- equivalently, $a_i = a_0 + a_1 b_i + e_i$ with $E(e_i \mid b_i, x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iT}) = 0$. Geometrically, the extrapolation line through $(E(a_i|1,0), E(b_i|1,0))$ and $(E(a_i|0,1), E(b_i|0,1))$ is intersected with the vertical line $a = E(a_i|0,0)$ to obtain $E(b_i|0,0)$, and with the -45 degree line $a + b = E(a_i|1,1) + E(b_i|1,1)$ to obtain $E(b_i|1,1)$.

Sufficient conditions (2.14)-(2.15): (i) $E(a_i|b_i) = a_0 + a_1 b_i$ (linearity), (ii) $x_{it} = g(b_i, c_{it})$ with cost shifters $c_{it}$ independent of $(a_i, b_i)$. The threshold-crossing illustration is $x_{it} = 1[b_i \ge c_{it}]$ --- the unit adopts when return exceeds cost.

### Moment conditions and instruments

The CRC model plus assumption (2.11) yields, on movers, the approximate IV regression model (Verdier's equation 3.4): $\hat{a}_i = a_0 + a_1 \hat{b}_i + r_i + z_{i,n}$, where $r_i = e_i + \sum_t u_{it}((1+a_1)(1-x_{it}) - a_1 x_{it})$ is a composite error and $z_{i,n} = o_p(1)$. Because $r_i$ correlates with $\hat{b}_i$ through the first-step noise, $\hat{b}_i$ is treated as endogenous. The moment conditions for the second step are:

- **Orthogonality/exclusion:** $E[r_i \mid x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iT}, i \in M_n] = 0$, i.e., treatment history is independent of the extrapolation error on movers.
- **Implied moments:** $E[(\hat{a}_i - a_0 - a_1 \hat{b}_i) \cdot \psi(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iT})] = 0$ for any function $\psi$ of treatment history, restricted to movers.

Stacking these moments across mover subgroups (indexed by $(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iT})$ patterns) gives an overidentified GMM system when $T \ge 3$. The second-step estimator is implemented as a GMM regression of $\hat{a}_i$ on $\hat{b}_i$ using the full vector of treatment-history indicators as instruments, on movers only. With $T=2$ it collapses to a Wald estimator. Verdier reports GMM with the optimal weight matrix rather than 2SLS because, under misspecification, the choice of weight matrix has first-order implications (Hall and Inoue 2003), and he documents visible divergence between 2SLS and GMM lines when the moments do not fit.

**Relevance condition (Assumption 3.b):** $E(b_i|0,1) \ne E(b_i|1,0)$. The two mover groups must have distinct average returns for the instruments to identify the slope $a_1$.

**Identification requirements:** $a_1 \ne 0$ for untreated-stayer ATE; $a_1 \ne -1$ for treated-stayer ATE (as $a_1 \to -1$, $E(a_i + b_i|1,1) \to a_0$, so $b_i$ no longer predicts $a_i + b_i$ and the relationship cannot be inverted).

### Robust extrapolation (with correlated cost shifters)

Generalized identifying assumption (2.18/3.7): $E(a_i \mid b_i, x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iT}, v_i) = m(v_i) + a_1 b_i$, where $v_i \in \{1,\ldots,N\}$ is an observed indexing variable for factors of selection into treatment that correlate with $(a_i, b_i)$. Equivalently, $a_i = e_v + a_1 b_i + e_i$ with village fixed effects $e_v \equiv m(v)$. The second step is a fixed-effects IV (i.e., within-cluster IV) regression of $\hat{a}_i$ on $\hat{b}_i$ using $x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iT}$ as instruments, fixed effects indexed by $v_i$, on movers only. The stayer ATE plug-ins become $\widehat{ATE}_{00,v} = (\bar{a}_{00,v} - \hat{e}_v)/\hat{a}_1$ (requires a stayer-overlap condition: each stayer's village must also contain at least one mover).

In the empirical application $v_i$ = village; 91% of untreated stayers live in a village with $\ge 1$ mover. For low-dimensional $w_i$, a linear-in-$w_i$ version can be estimated by IV regression of $\hat{a}_i$ on $(\hat{b}_i, w_i)$ using $(w_i, x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iT})$ as instruments (footnote 27).

### Optimal-IV formula

The paper reports GMM with the optimal (two-step efficient) weight matrix but does not derive a closed-form optimal-IV expression in the main text; the online appendix contains analytical asymptotic variance formulae that can be evaluated as sample analogues. Inference options: (a) cluster bootstrap applying both steps to each resample (Mammen 1992), clustered at cross-sectional unit for the simple extrapolation and at $v_i$ for the robust version; (b) analytical standard errors from the appendix formulae.

### Overidentification test (testing the extrapolation)

**Testability requirement:** With $T=2$, assumption (2.11) is saturated --- equations (2.12)-(2.13) define four parameters ($a_0, a_1$, plus $E(b_i|0,1), E(b_i|1,0)$) in terms of CRC-identified quantities with no degrees of freedom. So **at $T=2$ the extrapolation is untestable**. Testability requires $T \ge 3$, because then there are more than two mover profiles and the corresponding points $(E(a_i \mid x_1,\ldots,x_T), E(b_i \mid x_1,\ldots,x_T))$ provide overidentifying restrictions on the extrapolation line.

**Test statistic:** A standard Hansen $J$-type overidentification statistic following the second-step GMM regression. The degrees of freedom equal (number of moments) minus (number of parameters), which for the simple extrapolation is (number of distinct mover treatment-history profiles) minus 2 (for $a_0, a_1$), and for the robust extrapolation is (number of mover profiles $\times$ number of $v_i$ values usable) minus ($a_1$ plus the number of fixed effects). Details are in the online appendix. The test is a **pooled** overidentification test across moment conditions indexed by treatment history (and by $v_i$ in the robust case), not period-specific.

**Auxiliary CRC test:** Verdier also reports a separate overidentification test of the CRC restrictions themselves: using the partial residual $e_{it} = y_{it} - z_{it} g_t$, test $E(\Delta e_{it} \mid x_{it} = x_{it-1} = x, m_i = m) = 0$ for $t = 2,\ldots,T$, $x \in \{0,1\}$, $m \in \{0,1\}$. In the Kenya application this CRC-validity test gives p = 0.32 (no rejection). The extrapolation test gives p < 0.01 for the simple extrapolation (rejects) and p = 0.26 for the robust extrapolation (does not reject).

### Recovering stayer ATE from switcher-identified parameters

Once $(\hat{a}_0, \hat{a}_1)$ or $(\hat{e}_v, \hat{a}_1)$ are estimated from movers, stayer ATEs are plug-ins:

- Untreated-stayer ATE (simple): $\widehat{ATE}_{00} = (\bar{a}_{00} - \hat{a}_0)/\hat{a}_1$, where $\bar{a}_{00}$ is the sample mean of $\hat{a}_i$ among untreated stayers.
- Treated-stayer ATE (simple): $\widehat{ATE}_{11} = (\overline{a+b}_{11} - \hat{a}_0)/(1 + \hat{a}_1)$.
- Untreated-stayer ATE (robust): $\widehat{ATE}_{00,v} = (\bar{a}_{00,v} - \hat{e}_v)/\hat{a}_1$, averaged over villages containing untreated stayers.
- With controls $z_{it}$: the first-step regression partials out $z_{it} g_0$, and the IV regression is run on residualized $\hat{a}_i, \hat{b}_i$.

### Point vs set identification

Verdier's approach delivers **point identification** for stayer ATE under the extrapolation assumption (either simple or generalized). It is not a partial-identification / bounds approach. The paper notes that partial-identification methods (e.g., Mogstad, Santos, and Torgovitsky 2018) could be combined with these results to obtain tighter bounds on policy-relevant parameters, but the main paper does not pursue this.

## Findings

- **CRC model not rejected.** Overidentification test of CRC restrictions (strict exogeneity + parallel trends + time-constant treatment effects): p = 0.32.
- **Substantial mover heterogeneity.** Average returns for movers = 23% overall, but ranging from ~0% (movers not using hybrid in 2010) and 8-11% (early adopters who disadopted) to 28-37% (late adopters and movers not using hybrid in 1997). Monotone selection pattern consistent with hybrid-seed cost falling over time.
- **Simple extrapolation rejected.** $\hat{a}_1 = -0.491$ (SE 0.348), $\hat{a}_0 = 5.044$; plug-in returns for untreated stayers = 66%. But adding average distance-to-seller $\bar{d}_i$ as an additional exogenous covariate yields $\hat{a}_2 = -0.04$ with p = 2.8%, and the direct overidentification test has p < 0.01. Both pieces of evidence reject the simple extrapolation.
- **Robust extrapolation not rejected.** Village-FE-IV yields $\hat{a}_1 = -0.949$ (SE 0.329). Overidentification test p = 0.26. Plug-in untreated-stayer returns = 37% (vs 66% under non-robust). Close to Carter et al. (2017) RCT estimate of 41%. Since $\hat{a}_1 \approx -1$, treated-stayer ATE is not reported.
- **Sign of $a_1$ is negative in both specifications.** Low-productivity farmers have higher returns to hybrid seeds --- consistent with hybrid seeds compensating for deficiencies of particular growing conditions.
- **Implication for misspecification.** Under misspecification the choice of weight matrix has first-order effects; 2SLS and GMM lines differ visibly in the simple-extrapolation plot.

## Contribution

1. **Unification.** Shows that Lemieux (1998) and Suri (2011) implement the same linear extrapolation from diff-in-diff estimates. Provides the first mechanical representation in terms of quantities identified by the CRC model.
2. **Clarification of restrictiveness.** Documents that the Lemieux-Suri extrapolation requires independence of non-treatment-effect selection factors from baseline heterogeneity --- a restriction that is likely violated when cost shifters (e.g., distance to market) correlate with baseline productivity. Suri (2011) asserted the assumption was "not restrictive"; Verdier shows otherwise in her own data.
3. **Robust extrapolation.** Introduces a generalized extrapolation that conditions on an observed indexing variable $v_i$ for cost shifters, estimated as a fixed-effects IV regression.
4. **Testability result.** Establishes that the extrapolation is testable as a GMM overidentification test when $T \ge 3$ but not when $T = 2$. Provides the practical test.
5. **Extensions.** Accommodates additional control covariates more flexibly than Lemieux-Suri (does not require $z$-history to have zero partial predictive effect on $a_i$). Sketches time-varying treatment effects extension and its equivalence to semiparametric DID (Abadie 2005).
6. **Empirical demonstration.** Reproduces Suri (2011) qualitatively but shows her 100% stayer-return estimate is driven by a misspecified extrapolation; the robust replacement gives 37% --- close to the 41% RCT benchmark.

## Replication feasibility

The data (TAMPA project, 1997-2010 waves) are the same public data used by Suri (2011), accessible via Tegemeo Institute. The online appendix at https://bit.ly/3fGURJe contains full analytical standard-error formulae and the $T > 2$ extensions. Estimation uses standard GMM IV regression and can be implemented in Stata (ivreg2 / gmm) or R (gmm package). Cluster bootstrap requires applying both steps to each resample. No closed-form optimal-IV matrix is needed --- two-step efficient GMM suffices. Unbalanced-panel handling in the first step is documented: units with $\ge 3$ waves enter $g_t$ estimation regardless of mover/stayer status; units with exactly 2 waves enter only if stayers. Overidentification test p-values are computed directly from the GMM $J$-statistic.

If $x_{it}$ were continuous without perfect persistence, stayers would form a subpopulation of mass zero, which is the case considered in Chamberlain (1992)." (p. 3, footnote 8)

Secondly, the treatment effect is assumed to be constant over time (up to conditional mean zero shocks), which, for instance, rules out so-called dynamic treatment effects... - "The robust extrapolation discussed in this section allows for dependence between the cost of using hybrid seeds and productivity, provided that this dependence originates from village specific cost shifters only. This accounts, for instance, for a farmer's distance to the nearest seed seller being correlated with a farmer's productivity since there is little variation in distance to the nearest seed seller across farmers who live in the same village, so that this cost shifter can be taken as common to all farmers who live in the same village." ### Methodological

- "Both Lemieux (1998) and Suri (2011) highlight the restrictions imposed by the CRC model (2.4) when discussing their empirical model, without emphasizing the restrictions imposed by (2.11). ... For instance, Suri (2011) states that assumption (2.11) is not restrictive (p. 181)." (p. 8 and footnote 17)
- "The appendix shows that the methods implemented by Lemieux (1998) and Suri (2011) can indeed be represented as the linear extrapolation depicted by Figure 1. This result provides a simple mechanical representation of these methods in terms of quantities estimated by difference-in-differences that was, to our knowledge, previously unknown." (p. 7)
- "Recall from section 2.2 that the CRC model can be equivalently written as a set of restrictions on the conditional first moments of baseline heterogeneity and treatment effects. Therefore it is likely that one cannot learn about non-linearities in the relationship between baseline heterogeneity and treatment effects without imposing stronger restrictions than the assumption of mean independence of the CRC model (2.4)." (p. 9)
- "we consider the case where the factors of selection into treatment other than treatment effects may be correlated with baseline heterogeneity or treatment effects, but where this correlation is due to factors of selection that originate from observed sources. ... We assume that the cost of treatment $c_{it}$ can be written as [EQUATION NOT EXTRACTED at p. 10]: where $w_i$ is observed while $n_{it}$ is unobserved." (p. 10)
- "in section 3.3 below we discuss in detail the case where $w_i$ is discrete but takes a number of values that is proportional to sample size, which is the case encountered in our empirical application and leads to a fixed effects estimation approach." (p. 11)
- "The restriction imposed by (2.23) is stronger than the restriction imposed by the simple extrapolation identifying assumption (2.11) since, in addition to imposing that treatment status history $x_{i1},\ldots,x_{iT}$ has no partial predictive effect on baseline heterogeneity $a_i$, it imposes that the control covariates $z_{i1},\ldots,z_{iT}$ also do not have a partial predictive effect. ... This additional restriction is not necessary for identifying ATE for stayers, as seen from the discussion above, and may be likely to be violated in many applications." (p. 12)
- "The first step of our estimation procedure, similarly as in Chamberlain (1992), consists of an OLS regression of the outcome variable $y_{it}$ on indicator variables for each cross-sectional observation, $d^j_{it} = 1[i=j] \; \forall j = 1,\ldots,n$; the interaction of these indicator variables with treatment status, $x_{it} d^j_{it} \; \forall j = 1,\ldots,n$; and indicator variables for each time period, $1[t=s] \; \forall s = 2,\ldots,T$, if the CRC model (2.4) without additional covariates is estimated, or the control covariates $z_{it}$ if the CRC model (2.20) with control covariates is estimated." (p. 13)
- "This first estimation step then yields estimates of time effects $f_t$, $\hat{f}_t$, for every time period $t = 2,\ldots,T$. ## Gaps and limitations

- **Linearity not relaxed.** The extrapolation assumes $E(a_i | b_i)$ is linear. Verdier notes one cannot learn about non-linearities from CRC first-moment restrictions alone; relaxing would require distributional assumptions as in Arellano and Bonhomme (2012).
- **Extrapolation untestable at $T = 2$.** The overidentification test requires $T \ge 3$. A migration application with only two waves cannot use Verdier's overidentification framework.
- **Unobserved cost shifters invalidate robust extrapolation.** Any cost shifter that varies within $v_i$-clusters (not just across) and correlates with $(a_i, b_i)$ would violate the robust extrapolation assumption. Only cluster-level cost shifters are absorbed.
- **$a_1 \approx -1$ blocks treated-stayer ATE.** In the Kenya application the robust estimate of $a_1 = -0.95$ is too close to $-1$ to report treated-stayer (always-hybrid) ATE, so only one stayer subpopulation is studied.
- **Point identification under assumption, not under weaker conditions.** The paper does not pursue partial-identification approaches that could accommodate weaker restrictions (e.g., Mogstad, Santos, and Torgovitsky 2018).
- **GMM misspecification.** Under misspecification (evident in the Kenya case for the simple extrapolation), 2SLS and GMM differ substantially; the paper illustrates but does not fully resolve the choice of weight matrix.
- **Binary (or discrete) treatment only.** Results extend to discrete treatment but not directly to continuous treatment without perfect persistence (where stayers are measure zero).
- **Relevance condition requires heterogeneous mover types.** Assumption 3.b ($E(b_i|0,1) \ne E(b_i|1,0)$) is essential; settings where both mover groups have similar average returns cannot identify $a_1$.
- **Inference requires two-step bootstrap.** Valid inference requires applying BOTH first and second steps to each bootstrap resample; naive second-step-only bootstraps are inconsistent.
